What breaks a tool-call loop

Gemma 4 31B, run as a coding agent with thinking off, often sends the same failed call dozens of times in a row. Since October 12 we have tested a dozen levers on these loops, one step ahead and over whole tasks. The loop is the agent copying its own earlier calls. What the tool results say barely matters. What the history looks like decides it. And ending a loop does not, by itself, solve the task.

In brief

  • Copies cause copies, if they are identical. At a fixed history, adding copies of a failed search raises the chance of one more from 0.2% to 10% (two copies) and 43% (four). Re-spelled copies of the same search stay near 3%.
  • The results don’t matter; the history does. Varying IDs on the repeated results, or a faithful “found zero matches” note, leave the copy where it was. Deleting the earlier copies cuts it from 0.79 to 0.07 and turns most copies into escapes.
  • The empty-thought block is a pattern break. Put the same four tokens before every earlier call and the copy comes back in full.
  • Temperature 1 stops loops starting, not locking in. Stale calls fall from 39% to 20% of all calls and loop tasks from 25% to 9%. Yet after three identical calls, a fourth still follows 87% of the time, and resolution is unchanged (32.6% against 33.0%).
  • Nothing inside a loop ends it. At T = 0.2, none of 58 runs of ten or more copies stopped by itself: 43 ended at the harness’s budget warning, 14 at a context compaction. Tasks that T = 1 un-looped were solved no more often (9 against 10).

The agent and its loops

The agent is Gemma 4 31B (int4, quantization-aware trained) inside the coding harness of the Gemma 4 agent competition on Kaggle. It works on real issues from four Python repositories, fastapi, requests, httpx and rich: 109 tasks, 70 from the development set and 39 from rich. Thinking is switched off, and it samples at temperature 0.2, top-p 0.95, top-k 64. At each step it reads the whole history (the task, every earlier call, every result) and writes one tool call. The harness runs it and appends the result. A task gets 60 calls and four minutes.

When a task fails, the trace often ends in a loop: one call, sent again and again, getting the same answer each time. Most loops are searches that find nothing. In the control arm, once the agent has sent the same call twice in a row, a third follows 77% of the time, and after three or more, another follows 96% of the time. Inside a tool-call loop walks through one such loop call by call. This post collects every ablation since: what each lever does, one real example of it escaping or failing, and what we would try next.

Terms

Copy
The same call as the one just before, with the same arguments.
Return
A call the agent already sent earlier, but not just before.
Stale call
A copy or a return with no file edit since the earlier one. “Other stale” means a stale call other than the one being measured.
Escape
A well-formed call that isn’t stale. It is one step ahead, not yet progress.
p(c)
The probability the model gives to sending call c once more. “Exact” is the model’s own probability. “At T = 0.2” applies the run’s sampling, which pushes values towards 0 or 1.
Lock-in
In whole runs, the chance that the next call is one more copy, given how many identical calls came just before.

Two ways to test a lever

One step ahead. Take a recorded state, meaning the history just before a call. Edit the context in one way, then ask the same weights what comes next: score p(c), and draw three calls at the run’s sampling. Everything else stays fixed, so any difference is the edit’s doing. Round 1’s forced thought, G1 and G2 work this way.

Over whole tasks. Run all 109 tasks with the lever on, against a control arm in the same setting, and compare the traces and the resolved tasks. This catches what one step can’t: loops that start somewhere else, budget spent, tasks solved. Round 1’s always-block run and G3 work this way. One run’s resolved count moves by about two tasks from noise alone, so trace measures come first.

Round 1 · October 12

Where the questions came from

Round 1 tried two levers. The first forces a real thought: the thought is opened for the model and can’t close for 32 tokens (we call it T-open). At looping states where the model, free to think, had skipped the thought, 58% of the forced-thought calls escaped, against 11% (114 states in 36 tasks; +47 points, 95% interval 33 to 61). Where the model chose to think, forcing a thought changed nothing (62% against 63%). Figure 1 shows the hardest case: third-or-later copies where the model wrote no thought.

The second lever, the always-block template, forces the four-token empty thought before every call, over whole tasks. Runs got shorter and started more often, and the arm solved the same 37 of 109 tasks as the control. Both results left one question open: what exactly is the model copying? Round 2 took the loop apart one piece at a time.

G1 · October 14

Copies cause copies, if they are identical

If a loop reinforces itself, adding copies to a fixed history should raise the chance of one more. G1 takes 60 histories in 56 tasks, each ending just after the first time an empty search ran. Every arm adds blocks that carry the identical empty result, so the arms differ only in the call:

  • dose2, dose4: the same call again, once or three times;
  • para2, para4: one or three re-spellings of the same search (swapped quotes, long flags, -e, a dropped trailing slash), each keeping grep’s empty output;
  • var2: a different empty search the agent made later in the task (24 histories).
  1. Identical copies raise the next copy. p(c) goes from 0.002 to 0.098 to 0.434, and sampled copies from 0% to 12% to 41%. In the model’s own probabilities it rises with each dose in 59 of 60 histories, by a median 2.5 log-odds per added copy between two and four.
  2. Re-spellings don’t. Three re-spelled copies add as many tokens as three identical ones and leave p(c) at 0.038. Seeing the same failure four times isn’t enough. The whole call has to recur.
  3. It is far below the closed loop. Two forced copies give a third about 10% of the time; in real runs, 77%. Four give 43%, against 96%. So the copies alone don’t explain the curve. Which histories end up looping matters as much. G2 puts a size on that.

G2 · October 15

The results don’t matter, the history does

G1 changed the call and kept the result. G2 keeps every call and changes the results, and adds three edits to the history. It uses 101 repeat states in 23 tasks: each ends just after an empty search c that repeats an identical earlier call with the identical result, with no file edit since. 32 first failures serve as controls. Each state is rendered eight ways:

  • The results, a 2 × 2. Every repeated result gets an 8-digit ID: one shared ID, or a different ID on each, so the results stop matching. Crossed with that, a 14-token note: neutral (“The tool server returned this response to the agent in the current session.”) or faithful (“The search completed and found zero matches for this pattern in these paths.”). Neither note says what to do.
  • The history. dedup deletes c’s earlier copies. block ends the context with the empty thought. think_all puts the empty thought before every earlier call as well, and at the end.
  1. Edits to the results do nothing useful. Varying IDs moves p(c) by −0.005. The faithful note moves it by +0.020: slightly more copying, and 5 points fewer escapes. Round 1’s note and hint did move the copy (0.94 to 0.49 and 0.45 at third-or-later copies), but they told the model what to do (“Do not run it again”). A description of the failure doesn’t move the copy. An instruction does.
  2. Edits to the history do. Deleting the earlier copies cuts p(c) by 0.41. The empty thought at the end cuts it by 0.32.
  3. The block is a pattern break. With the empty thought before every earlier call too, p(c) is back where it started. At copy states the copy returns in full: 0.818, against 0.791 with no thought anywhere. The block works because the end of the context no longer looks like the moments just before the earlier copies. Put the same tokens there as well, and the match is back.
  4. Dedup and the block fail differently. At copy states the block mostly turns copies into other stale calls (+0.33) rather than escapes (+0.10). That is the fragmentation round 1 saw over whole tasks, one step ahead. Deleting the copies turns them into escapes (+0.41).
  5. Selection, measured. Deleting the copies takes a copy state back to roughly where its run began: c once, after the same history. There p(c) is already 0.074, against 0.009 at first failures. Copies alone (G1’s four-copy arm) give 0.31, the state alone 0.07, both together 0.79. Runs begin where the model already leans towards c, and the copies then lock it in.
  6. Returns are another regime. When c comes back after other calls, the model cycles over several stale calls: c 30% of the time, others 50%. p(c) barely moves, but dedup, the block and think_all each raise escapes by 18 to 26 points. The copies also suppress the model’s own empty thought: at copy states it opens one 11% of the time, and 58% once the copies are deleted.

Four states show how each edit works on a single history.

G3 · October 15–16, two passes

Over whole tasks, fewer loops start, but they still lock in

G1 and G2 look one step ahead. G3 asks two questions over whole tasks. Is lock-in an artifact of sampling at 0.2? And do round 1’s always-block results replicate on the v31 serving stack? Three arms ran on all 109 tasks: the control at T = 0.2, the always-block template at T = 0.2, and the control at T = 1.0 (Google’s recommended setting; only the temperature line of the agent’s zip changed). Pass 1 ran all three. Pass 2 repeated the two controls.

  1. T = 1 cuts the onset. Stale calls halve, copies fall from 28% to 12% of calls, and loop tasks from 25% to 9%. A copy right after a stale call becomes half as likely (0.100 to 0.052).
  2. Lock-in survives. Once a run is under way, T = 1 barely slows it. The model’s own preference does the locking; low temperature only hardens it. That matches G1 and G2, whose effects were in the exact probabilities all along.
  3. Resolution doesn’t move, and T = 1 has costs: 6.5 more timeouts per pass (+0.5 to +13.0) and 9.5 more tasks with a malformed call (+2.5 to +17.0).
  4. The block replicates its shape, not round 1’s stale drop. Copy starts double (0.025 to 0.050), lock-in at three or more falls 5.6 points and re-entry into a past loop rises 26 points, as in round 1. But the stale share doesn’t fall (+1.6 points), and loop tasks don’t either. Round 1’s “stale share falls 5–6 points” needs withdrawing.

One task, fastapi_13920, shows all three arms. The issue asks for PEP 695 TypeAliasType support by vendoring a helper, is_typealiastype, from the typing-inspection package. The helper isn’t in the repository yet, and the agent goes looking for it.

Nothing inside a loop ends it

Both examples end the same way, and so does almost every long run. On clean steps (no budget warning in the last result, no compaction before the next call), the control arm at T = 0.2 sent one more copy every time after five or more identical calls: 290 of 290 steps at five to nine, and 1,052 of 1,052 at ten or more. Every exit came from outside the loop.

Un-looping solves nothing on its own

T = 1 gives a natural test of what loops cost. Take the tasks that loop in a control pass and look at the same pass at T = 1.

The freed budget went into exploration, and exploration didn’t find the fix. Loop task-runs resolve 20% of the time in the control, against 37% for the rest, but the loop looks more like a symptom of being lost than the cause of failing. An agent that loops before its first edit hasn’t yet found where the change goes.

Putting it together

Across the three rounds the loop has a consistent shape. The model treats its own earlier calls as demonstrations. A copy needs the identical call (G1). It ignores what the results say (G2). It is broken by changing what precedes the next call, and restored by matching it again (think_all). Runs begin at states where the model already leans towards the call, and the copies then lock it in. Over whole tasks, sampling at T = 1 makes fewer runs begin but doesn’t stop the ones that do. At T = 0.2, nothing the agent writes ends a long run; only the harness does. A real thought breaks the copy one step ahead, and the model seldom chooses to think inside a loop.

What we would try next

The deadline is November 12, and the plan freezes experiments on October 27. In priority order:

  1. Dedup over whole tasks

    Collapse consecutive identical call and result pairs in what the model sees, against the v31 control, two passes. It is the one lever that turned copies into escapes rather than other stale calls, and G3 says exits only come from changes to the context, which compactions already make by accident. The main track’s token proxy, which edits every chat request, could carry it; a chat template might too. Measure the stale share, re-entry, and what the freed calls buy (distinct calls, source edits) before resolution.

    Takes G4’s slot, since G2 failed G4’s condition. About 3 A100-hours. Proposed, not built.

  2. A forced thought where the agent gets stuck

    Open a real thought when the same call arrives a second time. One step ahead this escapes 58–69% of the time where the model skipped thinking. The open question is whether an escape becomes progress over a whole task, since un-looping alone did not.

    Listed as out of scope for round 2 (a failure-triggered controller), so it needs a decision first.

  3. Where the freed budget goes

    A CPU-only audit of the existing T = 1 traces: in un-looped tasks, did the extra calls reach the right file and function, and what went wrong after the edit? If loops start before the agent knows where to edit, localisation is the real bottleneck, and loop-breaking alone can’t move resolution.

    No GPU. Uses files already copied locally.

  4. Exit distillation, only if the first item lands

    Train the 31B on loop contexts, with the call that dedup or the forced thought produced as the target, against its own copies as targets. A clipped-against-unclipped rollout comparison is not worth running as designed: success filtering already removes most loops (20 of 175 resolved task runs hold a run of three or more), and clipping removes the loop states a model would need to learn recovery from.

    Gated on the first item by about October 20, and on a trainer check on v31. Not decided.

  5. Cheap one-step follow-ups

    The missing cell of the thought 2 × 2: the empty thought before every earlier call but not at the end. If the match reading holds, the model writes the empty thought itself and copies through it. Also: score the empty-thought tokens alone at each G1 dose, and add G2’s copy/return split to loop_report.py.

    Under an A100-hour each.

  6. Generality

    86 of G2’s 101 states and 39 of G1’s 60 histories come from fastapi, and three tasks give a third of G2’s states. A 12B control run on v31 is the cheapest check on a second model, though any one-step claim there needs its own replay states.

    One A100 job per pass; the 12B has never been run in this harness.

What this doesn’t show

  • Progress. An escape is a fresh call one step ahead. Example 3’s escape searches a second file for a function that exists in neither.
  • Mechanism inside the model. “Match” describes behaviour. Nothing here looks at attention or activations.
  • Small effects on resolution. Two passes of 109 tasks can’t detect a change of one or two tasks.
  • Other wordings. Each note had one wording, by design. A stronger surface break than an 8-digit ID wasn’t tried.
  • Exact replication. G1 and G2 score v30 histories in the v31 serving environment. The replay reproduces the runs (mean p(c) 0.639 against a recorded copy rate of 0.634; AUC 0.95), but it is one step ahead.
  • Other agents. One model, one harness, thinking off.
