When Coding Agents Repeat: Persistence, Selection, and the 
Limits of Loop Breaking 
A study of repeated tool calls in Gemma 4's coding agent  
Jiarui Zha, zha.j@northeastern.edu 
Abstract 
We study why Gemma 4 31B, as a coding agent with thinking off, sends the same tool call again and 
again until its budget runs out. On 109 tasks in the harness of Kaggle's Gemma 4 Developer Agent 
competition, at least half the calls are repeats or malformed in a quarter of task runs, and these runs 
resolve 20% of the time, against 37%. Using one-step replays at fixed histories and whole-task runs 
under three levers, we obtain three findings. (1) The chance of one more identical call rises from 78% 
after two calls to 99% after ten even with a fixed request, showing that accumulating copies is 
unnecessary for increasing persistence. Fixed-history edits independently show that identical copies 
reinforce immediate repetition. (2) One-step escapes can be induced: where the model, free to think, 
chose not to, a forced thought lets the next call escape 58% of the time, against 11%. (3) None of the 
three whole-task interventions produced a detectable improvement in resolution under the tested 
budget. 
1 Introduction 
Coding agents often send the same tool call again and again until the budget runs out. In SWE-Gym, 
29–40% of zero-shot runs of open models repeat one action three times in a row (Pan et al., 2025), 
and harnesses such as OpenHands and Gemini CLI ship loop detectors. We met this failure while 
building an agent with Gemma 4 31B, thinking off, for Kaggle's Gemma 4 Developer Agent 
competition: in one early run, half the failed tasks (23 of 46) spent at least a fifth of their calls on 
repeats. 
Self-reinforcing repetition is known in text generation (Holtzman et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2022). After 
two identical actions, agents take a third 37% of the time (Liu et al., 2026), and branches of a research 
agent restarted with a fresh context sometimes land in a new “idea basin”, which their score then 
selects (Cai et al., 2026). We ask whether the chance of a repeat rises because of the copies or because 
runs start where the model is already near certain of the call, and whether breaking loops over whole 
tasks, with no grader to choose among the escapes, solves more tasks. 
We answer with one-step replays at recorded histories and with whole-task runs of three levers against 
a control. Our contributions are: 
(1) Persistence rises even with a fixed request. Continuation increases from 78% to 99% under the 
proxy, consistent with selection of persistent states; fixed-history edits separately establish 
reinforcement by identical copies. The two model sizes show similar continuation curves once 
copying has begun (§4). 
(2) In the 31B control at T = 0.2, no spontaneous exit occurred in 1,342 observed clean transitions 
after five or more identical, well-formed calls (§5). 
(3) The pattern of the context cues the copy, not what the results say; a forced thought breaks it where 
the model chose not to think (§6). 
(4) The three whole-task interventions alter repetition, but none produces a detectable improvement in 
resolution under the tested budget (§7). 
2 Setting and measures 
The agent is Gemma 4 31B (int4, w4a16) with thinking off, in the competition's harness, with the 
host's sample prompts plus rules for tool-call arguments. Each task allows 4 minutes, 60 tool calls and 
100 turns; sampling is T = 0.2, top-p 0.95, top-k 64. The tasks are the 109 of the competition's 129

public development tasks whose grading is sound (the reference patch passes and an empty patch 
fails), from fastapi, requests, httpx and rich. A run is resolved when the grader's tests pass. 
Whole-task runs compare a control with three levers, two passes each unless noted: T = 1, as Google 
recommends (Google, 2026); a chat template with an empty thought before every model turn, as a 
Hugging Face pull request proposes (Hugging Face, 2026; one pass); and a proxy that collapses each 
run of identical tool turns (same call, same result) to its first. The harness still runs every call, but 
while a run lasts the model gets the same request again. Two passes of Gemma 4 12B test generality. 
One-step replays score the next call at recorded histories that we edit (adding copies, rewriting results, 
forcing a thought open): the probability of the recorded call, and one to three sampled calls per state. 
The histories were recorded on an earlier serving stack; where we checked, the replayed copy rate 
matches the recorded one (0.639 against 0.634). 
A call is stale if it repeats an earlier one (same tool and arguments) with no successful file edit since, 
and a copy if that call came just before. A malformed call's arguments do not fit the tool. A loop task 
run has at least half its calls stale or malformed. After k identical calls, h(k) is the probability that the 
next call is identical. Figure 1b uses transitions following well-formed calls on clean steps: no budget 
warning in the last result and no compaction before the next call. For the proxy at k ≥ 2, the figure 
additionally requires that the entire current run was collapsed. Paired differences reported for all well-
formed steps use a separate population. The conventional exit hazard is 1 − h(k). An escape, one step 
ahead, is a sampled call that is neither stale nor malformed. Intervals are 95%, from resampling tasks. 
3 Loops are common and mark lost runs 
In the control, 38.6% of calls are stale and 28.0% are copies, and a quarter of task runs (54 of 218) are 
loop task runs. Most long runs repeat a search or a command: of the 116 runs of three or more 
identical calls, 39 are read-only searches, 50 other commands and 26 malformed calls. Loop task runs 
resolve 20% of the time (11 of 54), against 37% (61 of 164) for the rest. Within the 20 sometimes-
solved tasks that have both kinds of run, the runs that loop resolve 22.8 points less often (−41.1 to −
4.3). Which tasks loop is largely a property of the task: the two control passes share 15 of their 27 
loop tasks, against about 7 by chance. Gemma 4 12B loops in 44.5% of its task runs. These are 
associations: a run that is already lost may loop because it is lost. §7 tests whether removing loops 
helps.

4 Selected, then sealed 
In the control, a fresh call is followed by an identical one 2.4% of the time. After two identical calls 
the continuation probability is 0.68, after three or four 0.96, and from five on the agent continued at 
every clean step (Fig. 1b). Two mechanisms predict a curve that rises like this. Reinforcement predicts 
that added copies increase the chance of another copy at a fixed history. Selection also predicts 
increasing continuation: under a fixed request, states with higher repeat probabilities are more likely 
to survive to longer runs. The proxy tests whether the curve can rise without accumulating copies; the 
fixed-history edits test reinforcement directly.

A rising continuation probability alone does not establish reinforcement. Suppose each state s draws 
the same call again with a fixed probability p, as it does when the request is held fixed. Over the states 
where runs start, ℎ(𝑘)  =

𝐸[𝑝𝑘 − 1], and since 𝐸[𝑝𝑘]2  ≤  𝐸[𝑝𝑘 − 1] 𝐸[𝑝𝑘 + 1] (Cauchy–Schwarz), 
h(k) never falls with k, whatever the mix of states. This is analogous to frailty selection in survival 
analysis (Vaupel et al., 1979): more persistent states become enriched among survivors, so 
continuation rises and the corresponding exit hazard falls. Under a fixed request and stationary, 
independent draws within a state, the rise can arise through selection alone. Because the two arms 
reach different states and retain different survivors, their gap does not identify the fraction of the 
control curve caused by accumulating copies. 
With the request fixed, the continuation probability still rises: 0.78 after two identical calls, 0.87 after 
three or four, 0.93 after five to nine and 0.99 after ten or more. High continuation persists without 
accumulating copies. In a separate analysis of all well-formed steps, paired differences over tasks 
(proxy minus control) are +7.6 points (−8.9 to +22.4) at k = 2, −7.0 (−13.3 to −0.8) at k = 3–4, and 
−4.1 (−7.3 to −1.1) at k = 5–9. These estimates include warning and compaction transitions and are 
not differences between the clean, fully collapsed values in Figure 1b. The between-arm differences 
are consistent with a contribution from copies, which the fixed-history edits test directly. Among runs 
of three or more calls that ended before the task, own exits occurred in 43 of 91 proxy runs (47%) and 
16 of 107 control runs (15%; difference +32 points, +20 to +45). Runs of ten or more occurred 0.25 
times per task under the proxy versus 0.33 under the control, about three quarters as often. This ratio 
compares incidence across arms, not matched survival of the same runs. One step ahead, at 60 fixed 
histories after a search that found nothing, identical copies raise the probability of the same search 
from 0.002 with one copy to 0.43 with four. Re-spelled copies of it, with the same results, leave it 
near 0.03. The copies reinforce on their exact tokens. 
On clean steps, Gemma 4 12B copies a fresh call 5.8% of the time, versus 2.4% for 31B. A separate 
all-step comparison gives +3.2 points (+2.2 to +4.2). Once copying begins, the observed continuation 
curves are similar; the all-step paired intervals include zero, which does not establish equivalence. We 
did not run the proxy at 12B, so the fixed-request selection test is limited to 31B. 
5 Observed exits of long runs 
In the 31B control at T = 0.2, no spontaneous exit was observed after five or more identical, well-
formed calls on clean steps: 290 of 290 transitions continued at k = 5–9 and 1,052 of 1,052 at k ≥ 10. 
Of the 58 well-formed runs of ten or more, 43 ended after the harness's budget warning (“Only 10 tool 
call(s) remaining …”), 14 at a context compaction and one with the task. Neither exit helps much: 
after the warning at most ten calls remain, and after a compaction the agent sends the same call again 
30–41% of the time. With the request held fixed, long runs end the same way (31 of 54 at the 
warning), and Gemma 4 12B ends only 4 of its 119 runs of ten or more itself. These observed long 
control runs ended following a budget warning, at compaction, or with task termination; this 
descriptive classification does not by itself identify the causal effect of either intervention. Malformed 
runs and the 12B control can end spontaneously. 
6 What cues the copy 
A clearer result does not stop the copy. At 101 states where an empty search had just been repeated 
(23 tasks), we rewrote the repeated results in a 2 × 2 design. Varying their IDs changes the probability 
of the copy by −0.005 (−0.009 to −0.002), and a faithful note (“The search completed and found zero 
matches …”) raises it slightly, by +0.020 (+0.005 to +0.036; its added tokens alone: −0.004). An 
instruction does move it: a controller's note that the call repeated the one before, and to try a different 
approach, took the probability of a third or later copy from 0.94 to 0.49, at other states. So does a 
break in the pattern. At the 56 copy states, ending the context with an empty thought takes it from 
0.79 to 0.22; with an empty thought before every earlier call too, it returns to 0.82. The empty thought 
breaks the pattern; it does not switch the model's mode. 
The model's own thinking does not supply that break. Free to think, it wrote a thought at 72% of 
second copies but at only 23% of third or later ones. At 213 looping states in 44 tasks, we forced a

7 Breaking loops over whole tasks 
Each lever changes the loops (Table 1). T = 1 cuts the stale share of calls by 18.2 points and loop task 
runs from 24.8% to 9.2%, yet a run once under way still continues 87% of the time at three or more 
calls. The empty thought before every call makes runs nine calls shorter but more numerous, and the 
stale share does not fall. The proxy lets the agent end short runs itself, but the share of copies stays 
where it was.

None of the three interventions produced a detectable improvement in resolution under the tested 
budget. T = 1 un-looped 46 of the control's 54 loop task runs: the control solved 10 of those tasks and 
T = 1 solved 9, spending the freed budget on new calls and source edits. In 23 of 24 such tasks in the 
first pass, the control's first stale call came before its first source edit. In the early run of §1, only 6 of 
the 23 failures with many repeats were solved by any of nine other agent runs, about 2 of 70 tasks. 
The resolution intervals retain uncertainty about small and moderate benefits; the two-pass design 
does not establish equivalence. The large 12B contrast is readily observed, while the effects of 
improving a small number of task runs remain uncertain. The cross-run rescue count describes the 
tested runs and does not bound what a new intervention could recover. 
8 Related work 
Repetition in text. Generators repeat themselves more with each repetition in context (Holtzman et al., 
2020; Xu et al., 2022), and reasoning models loop at low temperature, where raising it is “a stopgap” 
(Pipis et al., 2026). Our tool-call loops show a similar rising curve, and holding the request fixed 
demonstrates that such a curve can arise without accumulating copies; fixed-history edits separately 
show reinforcement by identical copies. 
Agents copying themselves. Agents condition on their own errors (Sinha et al., 2026) and imitate their 
earlier responses (Wan et al., 2026). In models up to 1.7B, a failed call in context makes the same call 
likelier, mostly through its surface form (Gumaan, 2026); we find the same exact-token effect at 31B. 
On screened histories, agents take a third identical action after two 37% of the time (Liu et al., 2026); 

 
6 
ours does 68% of the time. Gemma 4 31B without thinking also loops in another harness (Y uan et al., 
2026). 
Repeats as symptoms. In SWE-smith, warnings, resampling and temperature bumps cut repeats while 
resolution fell, and the authors read repeats as “symptoms of the model's difficulty” (Yang et al., 
2025). Our three whole-task interventions provide further evidence that reducing repetition alone need 
not improve resolution, although their confidence intervals do not exclude useful benefits. Fork-and-
Flush escapes idea basins when fresh-context branches are scored and the best is kept (Cai et al., 
2026); a coding agent has no such score during a run, and removing the copies produced no detectable 
increase in resolution in our experiment. 
Detectors. OpenHands acts after four identical action–observation pairs, and Gemini CLI after five 
identical calls (OpenHands, 2026; Gemini CLI, 2026). At 31B, lock-in is near complete after three. 
9 Limits and implications 
The evidence comes from two sizes of one model family (int4), with thinking off, in one harness, on 
109 tasks from four repositories. The mechanisms and the levers were measured at 31B only; the 12B 
tests the descriptive claims. With two passes, the resolution estimates remain too uncertain to establish 
equivalence or exclude benefits affecting only a small number of tasks. The forced thought was tested 
one step ahead, never over whole tasks, and an escape one step ahead need not be progress. The 
probabilities we report are re-draws of the same call, not calibrated confidence, and nothing here 
looks inside the model. The empty-thought template and the proxy test mechanisms, not entries: a 
competition submission can change neither the chat template nor the requests. 
Three things follow for agent builders. Lock-in is near complete by the third identical call, earlier than 
common detectors act. Raising the temperature, breaking the pattern, and removing copies altered 
repetition without producing a detectable resolution improvement. The results are consistent with 
loops marking difficult states, while leaving open a causal contribution of repetition to failure. A lever 
worth testing next acts where the model chose not to think, or brings the agent information it lacks. 
Breaking a loop can free budget, but these interventions did not demonstrate improved task recovery 
under the tested conditions.
